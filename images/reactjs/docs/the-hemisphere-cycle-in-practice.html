The idea in short

Iain McGilchrist argues that the two brain hemispheres differ in how they attend to the world. Both take part in almost everything, including language and reasoning.

Right hemisphere

Broad and open

The right hemisphere attends broadly and stays open to what is new. It takes a thing in as a whole, in its context.

Left hemisphere

Narrow and focused

The left hemisphere attends narrowly and with focus. It grasps, names, separates and plans, which is what makes tools and maps possible.

He tells it through the story in the title of The Master and His Emissary. A wise master sends a trusted emissary to run his territory. The emissary is clever but sees less, comes to believe he is the master, and the territory suffers.

What matters for this doc is the order of work. Experience starts with the right hemisphere, passes through the left for clarity and structure, and returns to the right to be put back into a living whole. The sections below apply that loop to the cart example.

The healthy cycle

McGilchrist's healthy cycle runs from the right hemisphere to the left and back to the right.

Presence gives the first, whole picture. Representation makes it explicit and workable. The return checks the result against the whole, and the next pass begins there.

The cart example, step by step

The cart demo went through the loop once, and the check at the end taught us something the tree had not.

1

Presence

Right

The cycle begins with wording and a rough sketch, nothing more. The wording:

When I click Add to cart on a product, the number next to the cart in the header goes up. The summary shows the same number. Clear sets it back to zero.

It is ambiguous on purpose. It does not say which components exist, or what Clear should do to the items.

2

Representation

Left

The sketch became a tree: App, CartProvider, Header, CartBadge, Main, ProductList, Product and CartSummary. Each component got a role: provides, reads, writes, or passes through. createContext and useContext followed from that map. One provider owns count, and three kinds of consumer read it or change it.

3

Return

Right

The page ran. Clicking Add to cart in a Product changed the badge in Header, which sits in a different branch, with no props passed between them. The render counters showed Header, Main and ProductList staying still.

The check in step 3 is made against the original picture, not against the code. It behaves like the sketch, so the idea survived. It also showed something the tree alone did not: every consumer re-renders when the count changes, even Product, which only calls setCount. That new detail is what feeds the next iteration.

Where the emissary takes over

The loop breaks when the left-hemisphere step stops serving the picture and starts replacing it.

FailureWhat it looks like in the cartThe check that catches it
Skipping step 1Starting from "use context" because it is the technique, then inventing components to fit itCan you say in one sentence what the page should do when someone clicks?
Mistaking the map for the territoryA tree that looks right while nobody has clicked through the running pageDoes the page behave like the original sketch?
Skipping step 3Giving the header and the summary their own useState. Each works alone, but the badge and the summary disagreeClick Add to cart and compare every place the count appears
The tool becomes the goalPutting everything in context because it works, so every consumer re-renders on any changeWhich components actually need this state?
The diagram becomes the workAdding colours, toggles and legends to the tree while the page goes unopenedIs the diagram still answering a question the page raised?

None of these is a mistake of skill. Each is what narrow, confident attention does when nothing brings it back to the whole.

Using the cycle for the next iteration

The next iteration starts from a new ambiguous picture: a cart that holds real products with quantities and a total.

1 · Right

Presence

Sketch one cart line: product name, quantity, price, and a way to remove it. Then a total under the list. Notice what is still unclear, such as what Clear should do to quantities.

2 · Left

Representation

Replace the single number with an items array and a reducer with add, remove and clear actions. Predict the tree before touching it. We expect the same components, with only the shape of the context value changing.

3 · Right

Return

Run it and compare with the sketch. If the tree held, the first picture was right. If a component had to move, the sketch was missing something, and that is worth knowing.

Each pass starts from a picture a little less ambiguous than the last.

Caveats and sources

Treat this as a working method borrowed from a contested account, not as a claim about brain anatomy.

  • McGilchrist's book came out in 2009 from Yale University Press, followed by The Matter with Things in 2021. Reviews were mixed.
  • Critics, among them A. C. Grayling, Michael Corballis and Owen Flanagan, say the cultural conclusions go beyond what the neuroscience supports.
  • Even in an overview written by McGilchrist, the evidence is called divided for some kinds of attention, and the two modes are described as not absolute.
  • The three steps of presence, representation and return are his framing. The Wikipedia page describes the underlying idea as thought beginning in the right hemisphere, being processed for speech by the left, and having its meaning integrated again by the right.
  • The loop is useful here only if it helps the work. That can be checked on every iteration, whatever the neuroscience says.

The pages I opened name the master and the emissary but do not retell the parable. Its wording in the first section is from my memory of the book's framing, so check it against the book.
